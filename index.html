Every road needs a place to stop.

For a thousand years, the serai was where caravans rested — a courtyard, a fire, bread and tea for whoever walked in. We're building the neighborhood version: a cafe and deli where travelers of the daily kind eat well and leave slower than they came.

Why "Serai"

Named for the inns that fed the Silk Road.

A caravanserai was built a day's ride from the last one — close enough that no traveler slept hungry. Inside the arch: water for the animals, a cell to sleep in, and a shared courtyard where strangers from four directions ate from the same pots.

That's the brief for this shop. Food that's genuinely restoring, not just fast. A room where you can stay twenty minutes past your coffee without anyone hovering. Sandwiches wrapped tight enough to survive the rest of your journey, even if the journey is the 4:15 school run.

The kitchen leans on the serai routes: slow-braised meats, flatbreads, pickles and herbs, rice done with patience, cardamom in the coffee and mint in the tea.

serai
noun · from Persian sarāy, "palace, inn"

A roadside inn with a central courtyard, built along caravan routes across Asia, North Africa, and southeastern Europe, where travelers rested and recovered from the day's journey.

Our usage: the place you stop on an ordinary day and leave better fed than you expected.

A day at the serai

The room changes with the light.

First light

Bread comes out, coffee pots go on. Flatbread with honey and clotted cream for the early road.

Midday

The deli counter runs full tilt — wrapped rolls, boxes, soup in travel cups.

Afternoon

The slow hours. Mint tea, shortbread, a corner seat and nowhere to be.

Evening

The braises land. Plates, not wrappers; conversations, not laptops.

House customs

Old rules of the road, kept.

Caravanserais ran on customs, not menus. We're keeping the ones that still make sense.

  1. Water is free, always.

    A jug and glasses by the door. No purchase, no questions.

  2. No one leaves hungry.

    If the day's bread is left at close, it goes with whoever's last in, free.

  3. Stay as long as you like.

    One tea buys the seat for the afternoon. The serai never rushed its guests.

  4. Strangers share the long table.

    One big table down the middle. Sit where there's room; that's the point of it.
